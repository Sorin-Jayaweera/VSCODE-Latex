## Postulates of QM

1) Linearity 
The state of a system is completely specified by a vector in Hilbert space
we have a ket like $\ket{\psi}$ or $\ket{\phi}$ where $\braket{ \psi | \psi }=1$
$\ket{\chi}=a\ket{\psi}+b\ket{\phi}$
$\bra{\chi}= \bra{\psi}a^{*}+\bra{\phi}b^{*}$

2) Physical observables correspond to Hermitian operators
$\hat{x},\hat{P},\hat{H},\hat{J}_{x},\hat{J}_{y},\hat{J}_{z}, \hat{J}^{2}$
$\hat{A}^{\dagger}=\hat{A}$

States of definite $\hat{A}$ are eigenstates of $\hat{A}$ with eigenvalue $a$, which is what you would measure

3) Symmetries (rotation, translation)
Correspond to unitary operators. These operators have an algebra with commutation relations  identical to classical symmetries/transformations

Commutations tell us how to combine symmetries, etc, but is not QM - its classical geometrical stuff. That is imported into the symmetries of unitary operators. Rotations are the only nontrivial things. 

4) Each symmetry is generated by a Hermitian operator connected to its Nother pair
$$
\begin{align}
\hat{R}(\theta \vec{n} )= e^{\frac{-i}{\hbar}\theta \vec{J}\cdot \vec{n}} \\
\hat{T}(\vec{a}) = e^{\frac{-i}{\hbar}\vec{\hat{p}} \cdot \vec{a}} \\
\hat{U}(t) = e^{\frac{-i}{\hbar}\hat{H}t}
\end{align}
$$

5) Measurement.
When a measurement is made of observable $\hat{A}_{1}$
* You get one of the eigenvalues of $\hat{A}$
* Each happens with a probability $\left| \braket{ a | \psi } \right|^{2}$
* After, the state is now $\hat{P}_{a}\ket{\psi}$ or $\ket{a}$ if there is only one state with this eigenvalue

As a consequence, the expectation value
$$
\begin{align}
\left< A \right> = \bra{\psi} \hat{A}\ket{\psi} 
\end{align}
$$


## Rotators and Generators?

Rotators: 
Up to all cyclic permutations (shift each x/y/z left and wrap around)
$$
\begin{align}
[\hat{J}_{x},\hat{J}_{y}  ] = i\hbar \hat{J}_{z} \\
\end{align}
$$

We can combine that with the fact that these generators make rotations in a quantum way to get interesting stuff.

3D rotations: 
$$
\begin{align} \\
\text{ lets rotate about the } \vec{z} \text{ axis }\\
\mathbb{S}(\phi \vec{k})= \begin{pmatrix}
\cos \phi & -\sin \phi & 0 \\
\sin \phi & \cos \phi & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

We could do the same about the $\vec{x}$ axis
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{S}(\phi \vec{i})= \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \cos \phi & -\sin \phi \\
0 & \sin \phi & \cos \phi
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
$$
Or if we rotate around $\vec{y}$ and leave that unchanged
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{S}(\phi \vec{j}) = \begin{pmatrix}
\cos \phi  & 0 & \sin \phi \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
-\sin \phi & 0 & \cos \phi
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

We can now consider the Taylor expansion of these for small $\phi =\Delta \phi$. The first order cancels out, so we have second order.
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbb{S}(\Delta \phi \vec{k}) = \begin{pmatrix}
1- \frac{\Delta \phi^{2}}{2} & -\Delta \phi & 0 \\
+\Delta \phi & 1- \frac{\Delta \phi^{2}}{2} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
$$
This is the same for the others, do it yourself. 

Lets check rotation around x * rotation in y - rotation in y * rotation in x
$$
\begin{align}
\underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix}
 \,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \, 
\end{pmatrix} }_{ x }\underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix}
 \,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \, 
\end{pmatrix} }_{ y } - \underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix}
 \,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \, 
\end{pmatrix} }_{ y }\underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix}
 \,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \,  &  \\
\,  & \,  & \, 
\end{pmatrix} }_{ x }
\end{align}
$$
Because of the commutation relation, this becomes
$$
\begin{align}
\underbrace{ \mathbb{S}(\Delta \phi^{2}\vec{k}) }_{ \text{ z matrix} } + \mathscr{O}(\Delta \phi^{3}) 
\end{align}
$$

This is how rotations work classically. The ways that these work better hold for how the rotation generators work quantum mechanically. 

Lets be careful with operators. 

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{R}(\Delta \phi \vec{i}) \hat{R}(\Delta \phi \vec{j}) - \hat{R}(\Delta \phi \vec{j})\cdot \hat{R}(\Delta \phi \vec{i}) = \hat{R}(\Delta \phi \vec{k}) - \hat{\mathbf{I}} + \mathscr{O} (\Delta \phi^{3})
\end{align}
$$
We Taylor expand to first order.
$$
\begin{align}
\hat{R}(\phi \vec{n})= e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar} \phi  \hat{\vec{J}}\cdot \vec{n}} \to   1- \frac{i}{\hbar} \hat{\vec{J}}_{x} \Delta \phi + \frac{1}{2}\left( -\frac{i}{\hbar } \hat{J}_{x}\Delta \phi  \right)^{2} 
\end{align}
$$
We do this for every term, multiply out, do math. Some terms cancel, some don't.

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{J}_{x}\hat{J}_{y}\cancelto{  }{ \Delta \phi^{2} } - \hat{J}_{y} \hat{J}_{x} \cancelto{  }{ \Delta \phi^{2} } = i \hbar J_{z} \cancelto{  }{\Delta \phi^{2}  }  
\end{align}
$$

However, we do have a nice commutation relation
$$
\begin{align} \\
\hat{\vec{J}}^{2}   = \hat{J}_{x}^{2} +  \hat{J}_{y}^{2} +  \hat{J}_{z}^{2} \\
[\hat{\vec{J}}^{2},\hat{J}_{\text{ any x,y,z}} ]   = 0
\end{align}
$$
For things that do commute, there are simultaneous eigenstates of both, which we write as $\ket{\lambda,m}$

## Raising and Lowering Operators
This is the only weird operator that isn't Unitary, Hermition, or a Projection.

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{J}_{+}  & = \hat{J}_{x} + i \hat{J}_{y} \\
\hat{J}_{-}  & = \hat{J}_{x} - i \hat{J}_{y} \\
 \hat{J}_{+}^{\dagger} & =\hat{J}_{-}  
\end{align}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{J}_{z}(\hat{J}_{\pm }\ket{\lambda,m})= (m\pm 1 ) (\hat{J}_{z}\ket{\lambda,m}  )
\end{align}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
 \hat{J}^{2}\underbrace{(\hat{J}_{\pm }\ket{\lambda,m}  ) }_{ c_{\pm  }  \ket{\lambda,m\pm 1 } } = \hbar^{2} \lambda (J_{\pm  }\ket{\lambda,m}  )
\end{align}
$$
