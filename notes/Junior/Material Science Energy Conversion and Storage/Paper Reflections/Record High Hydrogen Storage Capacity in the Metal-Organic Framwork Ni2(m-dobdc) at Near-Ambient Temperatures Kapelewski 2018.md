- How do the results of this paper compare to what we would like in an actual car? Please compare it to the DOE goals listed in the paper (Table 1) and with conventional hydrogen gas storage methods.

The MOFs tested hit the 100 bar standard, but at cryogenic temperatures. This isn't good enough for use in actual cars, since that poses a significant problem (cars go through a huge range of temperatures). The ambient temperature ranges need to go from -40 to 85 degrees (c). 

- How do the MOFs create a higher concentration of H2 than H2 gas? What process?

They have dipole moments that are geometrically more similar to the condensed H2 than the H2 gas. By inducing a dipole, the H2 then locks onto the surface site of the MOF. 

    
- What characterization methods were used to verify how the MOF worked?
Powder Neutron Diffraction and Infrared Spectroscopy, with the dominant resonance peaks identified with either gaseous or adsorbed H2. 

- Referring to Fig. 1, what H2 storage curve would be most ideal for the device?
It would be best if the total uptake were high at high pressures and low at low pressues, since we want to pressurize to store, then heat and let out to release. We want an easy way to distinguish between the pressures of uptake and release, and have to store at or below 100 bar. It would be nicer if the slope were a lot stronger, so more uptake per change in pressure.
