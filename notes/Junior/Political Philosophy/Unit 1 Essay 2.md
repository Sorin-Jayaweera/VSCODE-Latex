On Political Obligation

Do citizens have a duty to obey the law - and if so, where does that duty come from?

  

The answer and its justifications might alter the way that we construct political societies, how strongly laws are enforced within a state, and what actions citizens might take against unjust conditions. In this essay, I will introduce major arguments for and against political obligation by prominent political philosophers John Simmons (does not believe in political obligation) and Christopher Wellman (who does). I will use the arguments of each in an attempt to push the stance of philosophical anarchism. ## Definitions

Political obligation is a duty to participate within a scheme of law or politics in a way that is not fundamentally justified by some other system, i.e. moral duties. People might follow laws out of a fear of punishment, but  Political Obligation is different - it is a fundamental, normative reason why all citizens of a state have to follow the laws other than being told to. Political obligation must not be determined internally within the system - i.e. the law can’t require people to follow the law, thereby making people follow the law. To really justify someone's obligation to a political society, we must find a fundamental reason to obey political organizations that are not rules of that system itself. Even further, the reasons justifying that every citizen without exception has political obligations must hold under scrutiny - i.e. it can’t apply to a subset of people in the state. If the justification were moral, one might also have to definitively prove that the morals are, in some sense, universal. If moral systems are relative (there is no true good, only perceptions of it), then people could have vastly different moral systems with which they could justify different actions, after which there is no way to have a single objective enforcement of justice. 

  

The ethics of states acting as dealers of justice and social regulators depend both on the existence of a fundamental method to measure justice, and a fundamental right of a state to enact punishment on citizens for breaking laws. Political obligation is different from moral obligation - someone might follow laws that are moral without believing in the stipulations of a state by the coincidence that those two value systems align. Political obligation is about duty to follow laws which do not have any reason outside of the fact that the state is enacting such laws, imposing on the freedoms of the state's citizens by punishing those who fail to comply. 

  

Simmons gives three main classes of arguments in favor of political obligation  - “Transactional”, “Associative”, and “Natural Duty” theories. Each is an entirely unique category of argument, which have commonly been employed to justify political obligation. 

  

Transactional theories argue that political obligation arises due to voluntary consent given by citizens to follow the laws of a political scheme. There are many ways that citizens might give consent - by accepting benefits, by constantly acting within the scheme, etc. However, there are nuances here – is it reasonable to expect that all citizens actually consent? That would require everyone to have genuine alternatives to the state that they were born in, even if they explicitly agree - and if they don’t explicitly, is it fair to suggest that they implicitly consent by accepting benefits that are generated by others participating within the public scheme? These questions are the core of Simmons’ argument against transactional theories, such as Fair Play, which I will show later. 

  

Associative theories are non-voluntary. They hold that political obligation arises due to the relationships and communities that someone is born into, simply out of the fact that they were born in that context. To reiterate, just by being born into a state that has social norms is enough to justify the obligation to obey those laws. Regardless of who you are or what you do, you are required to follow the rules because of the relationships you were born into. Family dynamics are a common example that associative theorists use - like how children have a duty to obey their parents, citizens must obey their states. People are involuntarily thrust into social contracts (nonvoluntarist contract theory), and are then bound to follow the contract and accept any punishment as justifiable. One of the most prominently accepted of these among political theorists is “nonvoluntarist contract theory”. A quick counterexample might jump to mind - did citizens of early 1900’s Germany have a duty to obey the commands of the Nazi Reich? Associative theories generate obligations entirely independently of the quality of the state, which opens them to unappealing consequences for citizens facing discrimination etc in unjust states. 

  

Natural Duty theories do not require consent, they apply to everyone everywhere. If someone is in a reasonably just state, then they must follow the rules of the state. Political obligations arise from a duty towards justice and each other instead of duties generated by consent or by a specific relationship towards other people. Justice, here, is hard to define. These struggle with questions that are less general though - what is special about a specific country, a specific law, on the assumption that laws vary with geographical locations (states). This “particularity” condition is at the core of arguments against Natural Duty theories, as we will see with Samaritanism. 

  

## Fair Play

I will now focus on discussion around the highly successful “Fair Play” theory, using the modified formulation of it by philosopher John Rawls in his essay “Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair Play”. When people work together in a mutually beneficial scheme, if someone is benefiting from the scheme then they have a duty to comply and work with the scheme in good faith. For a system to successfully count, it must be a constitutional democracy where all citizens work together to provide public goods which they all share. This depends crucially on several factors: - that a just constitution can be made, anyone suffering injustice that arises has a fair chance to change the laws despite being in a constant minority, that everyone is required to work to generate the benefits, that accepting the benefits requires someone to consent and enter the scheme, and that people still have an obligation to follow laws that they personally believe are not optimal – they are agreed on the constitution and rules of voting, and therefore must act as they wish others to behave if the roles were reversed. Fair Play is not a utility maximization, which might disenfranchise a minority for a large benefit of the majority; because no one would agree readily to be in the minority, discrimination can’t be part of a Fair Play system. It requires people to have as much liberty as possible without restricting the freedoms of other people, without any injustice towards any group. Even further, everyone must participate within the scheme, even if they are not all fundamentally required to produce the benefits to everyone. If a single person stops cooperating, the functioning of the state would remain almost entirely unchanged - but the person is then taking advantage of everyone else by using free benefits without cooperating. The free benefit is not something that could be shared by everyone - at least some people have to keep working - therefore the benefit “belongs in fairness to no one” (Rawls 1964, 122).  Fair play is a transactional theory – people have to voluntarily accept benefits to participate within the scheme. The scheme shouldn’t collapse entirely if some people don’t consent or cooperate, and anyone who voluntarily accepts benefits has to follow the scheme. 

  

Rawls’ formulation of fair play fights two issues with political obligation: unjust laws, and utility. 

Rawls summarizes in his paper: “... and if one has accepted the benefits of its working and intends to continue doing so, and if the rule enacted is within certain limits, then one has an obligation, based on the principle of fair play, to obey it when it comes one’s turn. In accepting the benefits of a just constitution one becomes bound to it, and in particular one becomes bound to one of its fundamental rules: given a majority vote in behalf of a statute, it is to be enacted and properly implemented” (Rawls, 122). Consider this scenario: there are two proposed laws - one that I think is just and the other not. If I vote for one that I believe is good, but the one I think is bad is enacted, do I have a duty to follow that law? For Rawls, this is based on the magnitude of the justice, and whether there is a reasonable ability for me to repeal the law, and the assumption that the current standing majority are acting on a good basis according to the principles of fair play. 

  

## Against Fair Play

Despite appreciation for the argument for Fair Play, Simmons argues that it is not sufficient to generate political obligation - Fair Play, as a consent theory, still only binds those who have genuinely consented. Rawls argues that by accepting the benefits of a scheme, one has become a member and thus has a duty to participate within the scheme. However, there are some types of benefits that can be classified as open (impossible to reject), or at least largely available (and therefore somewhat burdensome to reject). It is possible for people to strongly oppose these benefits and still (intentionally or not) receive their benefits as a free rider because the rest of the congregation decided to implement changes. Fair play isn’t just consent theory - if you steal benefits that you didn’t consent to being made, you still have to follow the regulations behind them - but it still fails for the requirement of consent because of the existence of open benefits. 

  

Further, people’s views are not all uniform - some may appreciate different aspects of life, and could thoroughly agree on what is optimal. An example that he uses: communal aesthetics (i.e. a garden) and people with varying preferences. If most people hate weeds, but someone else genuinely likes them and doesn't want them removed, then the person who likes weeds shouldn’t have an obligation to do the weeding. Therefore, the “benefit” is not for them, so they don’t have to participate if they don’t consent. Even if someone dislikes weeds, they might not value the absence of weeds enough to dedicate their time, which they would far rather be dedicating to something else (Simmons, The Principle of Fair Play, 21). This strongly positions the consent aspect of fair play as morally relative, since the duties to do things depend entirely on people’s own view points - so there isn’t holding power if people disagree. Simmons uses another example to separate consent from accepting benefits. Someone might strongly oppose a communal decision to do things if they don’t like the personal cost, but still want to use the result if it is already there. To be an insider in the fair play scheme, someone has to knowingly consent to the benefits and the work required, and genuinely perceive the benefits to be worth the personal cost to them. Simmons argues that most citizens are either those “who have not ‘accepted’ because they have not taken the benefits (with accompanying burdens) willingly, and those who have not ‘accepted’ benefits because they do not regard the benefits of government as the products of a cooperative scheme” (The Principle of Fair Play, Simmons, 25). If acceptance/consent are taken literally, then people don’t fall into the category of those who are bound to the laws of the government.  Just because people are following the laws and accepting benefits from living within a society does not mean they have actually given consent. Fair play, while highly attractive, is not sufficient to generate universally applying political obligations. People might explicitly agree to a social contract and agree to fair terms and the repercussions, but transactional theories can not generate a political obligation to all citizens within a state.
