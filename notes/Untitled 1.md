An idea that I spent a large part of the first unit grappling with was the usefulness of distinction between responsibility and what onus there is on any given individual to do a given set of tasks. Realistically I can't force someone to do something against their will, only try to stop them from doing something that they are doing (hard to apply positive pressure, a lot easier to do backward looking or negative preventative pressure). Because of this, the conversation around "duty" and "forward looking responsibility" and other notions of things that people should be doing doesn't seem to hold much weight to me - as much as we can talk about these things, it is hard to motivate people to actually doing them (I totally agree with Delmas that we have a huge range of things that we can do to help people, but I don't think its useful to tell people that they have an infinite responsibility that they aren't living up to). Because of this, I don't fund duty and responsibility compelling things to hold people to. Instead, I want to think about people's nature as reactionary (people are themselves systems that take inputs from other people and output a response, which influences the people around them) to the situations around them and the local incentive structures. 

In government, the "incentives" are blame -> punishment based with law as justification. We've had a lot of conversation about blameworthiness for actions that aren't tied to punishment, which feels like a weak reprimand - i.e. young saying that all citizens of Nazi Germany were to blame, even if only those directly involved were responsible. We've made a basis for some voluntary things, by way of task responsibility, but this still doesn't apply any pressure to people who are not directly involved in an injustice to actually act to fight the injustice - so again, we seem to laud ourselves as superior for recognizing blame, without getting the message to an audience in a way that it would matter. To me, the main reason for those conversations is only to justify punishment or punitive measures in either direction (from citizens to rebel state or state to punish citizens), but both people and governments are fallible. It is important to understand definitely - i.e. it is central to the conversation of civil and uncivil disobedience for justified causes that we spent so long on with Delmas, and I can see a lot of injustice being discovered to rally people around. However, my main thought process is on what people should actually do to rebel - somebody somebody somebody somebody should do something. The Both And framework tells us that actions are both structural and individual, but that hasn't really solved the main thing that someone would be thinking - they have a limited bandwidth and have a limited set of things to do. Obviously there isn't a prescriptive answer for people to dispel their theoretical duties (there are too many crucial roles to enumerate, anything would be grossly oversimplified or have gargantuan amounts of context).
